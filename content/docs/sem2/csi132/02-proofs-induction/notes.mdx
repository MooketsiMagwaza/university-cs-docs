## Proofs and Induction

Start a proof by identifying the hypothesis and the exact conclusion. Definitions are tools: expand the definition of even, divisible, subset, injective, or continuous before choosing a strategy.

## Main strategies

In a direct proof, assume the hypothesis and transform it through justified steps to the conclusion. To prove $P\to Q$ by contrapositive, prove the equivalent statement $\neg Q\to\neg P$. To prove a universal statement false, one counterexample is enough. A contradiction proof assumes the negation of the desired conclusion and derives an impossibility.

Proof by cases is useful when an object naturally splits into alternatives, such as an integer being even or odd. In every case, state why the cases are exhaustive.

## Mathematical induction

To prove $P(n)$ for all integers $n\ge n_0$, verify the base case, assume $P(k)$ for an arbitrary $k\ge n_0$, use it to prove $P(k+1)$, and then state the quantified conclusion. For $1+2+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2$, the step separates the first $k$ terms, applies the hypothesis, and simplifies to $(k+1)(k+2)/2$.

Strong induction allows the inductive step to use all earlier cases $P(n_0),\ldots,P(k)$. It is useful when a new object is built from several smaller objects, such as a factorisation or recurrence.

## Quantifiers and precision

The order of quantifiers matters. “For every $x$ there exists $y$” does not mean the same as “there exists $y$ for every $x$.” State domains explicitly and avoid using an example as if it proved a universal claim.

## Proof checklist

- Are all variables introduced and in the correct domain?
- Did I use the hypothesis, or silently assume the conclusion?
- Is each implication reversible when I claim equivalence?
- Are cases exhaustive and non-overlapping where needed?
- Does the final sentence match the quantified claim?
