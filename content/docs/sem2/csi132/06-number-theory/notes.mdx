## Number Theory

For integers $a$ and $b$, $a\mid b$ means there is an integer $k$ such that $b=ak$. Divisibility is transitive: if $a\mid b$ and $b\mid c$, then $a\mid c$.

## Division algorithm and gcd

For $a\in\mathbb Z$ and positive $b$, there are unique integers $q,r$ with $a=bq+r$ and $0\le r<b$. The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly replaces $(a,b)$ with $(b,a\bmod b)$ until the remainder is zero. The last non-zero remainder is $\gcd(a,b)$.

For example, $252=2(105)+42$, $105=2(42)+21$, and $42=2(21)+0$, so $\gcd(252,105)=21$. Back-substitution expresses the gcd as an integer combination of the original numbers, which supports modular inverses.

## Congruence

The statement $a\equiv b\pmod m$ means $m\mid(a-b)$. Congruences can be added and multiplied, so reduce large values before calculating. Division is not automatically valid: to cancel $c$ modulo $m$, require conditions such as $\gcd(c,m)=1$.

The prime factorisation of every integer greater than one is unique apart from order. It gives divisors, gcds, and lcms: take minimum prime exponents for a gcd and maximum exponents for an lcm.

## Common mistakes

- Allowing a negative or too-large remainder in the division algorithm.
- Treating congruence as ordinary equality.
- Cancelling a factor modulo $m$ without checking invertibility.
- Forgetting that $1$ is not prime.
