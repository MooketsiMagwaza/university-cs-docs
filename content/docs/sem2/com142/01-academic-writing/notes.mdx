## Academic Writing

Academic writing is controlled reasoning for a particular reader. It answers a question, makes its scope clear, supports claims with evidence, and signals the relationship between ideas.

## Purpose and audience

Before drafting, identify the assignment verb and the reader’s needs. Define specialised terms, state assumptions, and give enough context for the argument to be understood without the lecture conversation around it. Formality does not mean using complicated words; precision and economy are more important.

## Claims and evidence

Separate a claim from the evidence that supports it and from your interpretation of that evidence. Qualify claims when the evidence is limited: “the results suggest” is more accurate than “the results prove” when the study is observational.

## Academic voice

Prefer concrete subjects and strong verbs. Remove filler, unsupported generalisations, and vague references such as “this shows” when the reader cannot tell what “this” means. First person may be appropriate when describing your method or reflection, but follow the course convention.

## Sentence-level clarity

Keep the main subject and verb visible. Use parallel structure in lists and compare like with like. A paragraph should normally develop one controlling idea; if it contains two unrelated claims, divide it or revise the topic sentence.

## Checklist

- What question does the writing answer?
- What is the central claim?
- Does every major claim have relevant support?
- Are limitations and alternative views acknowledged?
- Can a reader follow the structure from headings and transitions?
