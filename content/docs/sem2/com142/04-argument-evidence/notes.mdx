## Argument and Evidence

An argument is a claim supported by reasons and evidence. A thesis should be specific enough to guide the essay and contestable enough to require support; “technology is important” is a topic, not an argument.

## Building the chain

For each major point, ask: What am I claiming? What evidence supports it? What warrants the connection? What limitation or alternative explanation should be considered? The warrant is often the missing explanation between a quotation and the conclusion drawn from it.

## Evidence quality

Relevant evidence addresses the claim; sufficient evidence supports its scope; credible evidence comes from an appropriate source; and current evidence is needed when the subject changes over time. A single striking example may illustrate a possibility but cannot establish a general rate.

## Counterarguments

Represent an opposing view accurately, identify the evidence or reasoning behind it, and respond by qualification, rebuttal, or showing why your evidence has greater explanatory power. Acknowledging a limitation can make a thesis more credible when the conclusion remains justified.

## Reasoning checks

Watch for hasty generalisation, false cause, false dilemma, circular reasoning, appeal to popularity, and attacks on a person rather than an argument. Do not label a fallacy as a substitute for explaining the specific weakness.

## Evidence integration

Introduce a source, quote or paraphrase only the relevant part, cite it, and explain its role. Never end a paragraph with an unexplained quotation. The reader should be able to tell which words are yours and what conclusion you draw from the source.
