## Applications of Differentiation

Critical numbers occur where $f'(x)=0$ or where $f'(x)$ is undefined, provided $x$ is in the domain. They are candidates for local maxima and minima, not automatic extrema.

## Increasing and decreasing

On an interval, $f'(x)>0$ indicates that $f$ is increasing and $f'(x)<0$ indicates that it is decreasing. Make a sign chart around critical numbers. A change from positive to negative gives a local maximum; negative to positive gives a local minimum.

The second derivative describes concavity. If $f''>0$, the graph is concave up; if $f''<0$, it is concave down. An inflection point requires a change in concavity, not merely $f''=0$.

## Absolute extrema

For a continuous function on a closed interval $[a,b]$, evaluate $f$ at every critical number in the interval and at both endpoints. The greatest value is the absolute maximum and the least is the absolute minimum.

## Optimisation method

1. Draw and label the situation.
2. Define the quantity to maximise or minimise.
3. Use the constraint to write it in one variable.
4. State the feasible domain.
5. Find and classify critical points, including endpoints.
6. Report the answer with units and interpret it.

For a rectangle with fixed perimeter $P$, $A=x(P/2-x)$. Differentiating gives $A'=P/2-2x$, so the stationary value occurs at $x=P/4$ and produces a square.

## Linear approximation

Near $x=a$, $f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$. This tangent approximation is useful when $x$ is close to a value with a known function value, but the error grows as the point moves away.
