## 3.2 Law of Total Probability and Bayes' Theorem

When an event can occur through multiple distinct pathways, we can calculate its total probability by summing the probabilities of these mutually exclusive pathways. Bayes' Theorem then allows us to reverse this logic, updating the probability of a specific pathway given that the event has occurred.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 3.2.1: Law of Total Probability (LoTP)"
  description="Calculates the total probability of an event by partitioning the sample space."
>

If events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ form a partition of the sample space $S$ (they are pairwise mutually exclusive and exhaustive, meaning $\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = S$), then for any event $B$:
$$
P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: Law of Total Probability</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Partition Event B</StepTitle>

    Since $A_i$ partition $S$, we can write $S = \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i$. 
    Therefore, $B = B \cap S = B \cap \left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n (B \cap A_i)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Establish Mutual Exclusivity</StepTitle>

    Because the sets $A_i$ are pairwise disjoint ($A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$), the intersected events $(B \cap A_i)$ are also pairwise disjoint.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom III (Additivity)</StepTitle>

    The probability of the union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities:
    $P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(B \cap A_i)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Substitute the Multiplication Rule</StepTitle>

    By the definition of conditional probability, $P(B \cap A_i) = P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)$. Substituting this yields the final formula:
    $P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 3.2.2: Bayes' Theorem"
  description="Reverses the conditional relationship to find the probability of a specific cause given an observed effect."
>

$$
P(A_k \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_k)P(A_k)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A_k)P(A_k)}{\sum_{i=1}^n P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)}
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Classical Application (Medical Testing)</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A disease affects 1% of the population ($P(D) = 0.01$). A diagnostic test is 95% accurate for those with the disease ($P(T^+ \mid D) = 0.95$) and has a 5% false positive rate for those without the disease ($P(T^+ \mid \overline{D}) = 0.05$). If a person tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease ($P(D \mid T^+)$)?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Identify Known Probabilities</StepTitle>

    $P(D) = 0.01 \implies P(\overline{D}) = 0.99$
    $P(T^+ \mid D) = 0.95$
    $P(T^+ \mid \overline{D}) = 0.05$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Total Probability of a Positive Test (Denominator)</StepTitle>

    Using LoTP: 
    $P(T^+) = P(T^+ \mid D)P(D) + P(T^+ \mid \overline{D})P(\overline{D})$
    $P(T^+) = (0.95)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Bayes' Theorem</StepTitle>

    $P(D \mid T^+) = \frac{P(T^+ \mid D)P(D)}{P(T^+)} = \frac{0.0095}{0.059} \approx 0.161$
    
    Despite testing positive, there is only a ~16.1% chance the person actually has the disease due to the low base rate (prior probability).
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
