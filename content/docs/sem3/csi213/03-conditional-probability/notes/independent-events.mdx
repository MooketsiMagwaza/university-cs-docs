## 3.3 Independent Events

Independence is a foundational concept in probability, indicating that the occurrence of one event provides no information about the occurrence of another.

<Card>

### Definition 3.3.1 (Statistical Independence)

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **Independent** if and only if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other. Mathematically, this is defined as:
$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)
$$
*(Note: This is equivalent to $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$ provided $P(A) > 0$).*

</Card>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 3.3.1: Mutual Exclusivity vs. Independence"
  description="A critical distinction: Mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are strictly dependent, not independent."
>

**Proof by Contradiction:**
Assume events $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive and $P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0$.
1. By mutual exclusivity, $A \cap B = \emptyset$, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
2. If they were independent, the definition requires $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$.
3. Since $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, their product $P(A)P(B) > 0$.
4. This creates a contradiction: $0 = \text{positive number}$, which is impossible.
Therefore, mutually exclusive events with non-zero probability cannot be independent. $\blacksquare$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Verifying Independence</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A single fair 6-sided die is rolled. Let $A$ be the event "rolling an even number" and $B$ be the event "rolling a number greater than 3". Are $A$ and $B$ independent?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Individual Probabilities</StepTitle>

    $A = \{2, 4, 6\} \implies P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5$
    $B = \{4, 5, 6\} \implies P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Joint Probability</StepTitle>

    $A \cap B = \{4, 6\} \implies P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Test the Independence Condition</StepTitle>

    Check if $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$:
    $P(A)P(B) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$
    Since $\frac{1}{3} \neq 0.25$, the condition fails. Events $A$ and $B$ are **dependent**.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Independence in Sequential Trials</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A fair coin is flipped twice. Let $H_1$ be the event "Heads on the first flip" and $H_2$ be the event "Heads on the second flip". Are $H_1$ and $H_2$ independent?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Individual Probabilities</StepTitle>

    $P(H_1) = 0.5$
    $P(H_2) = 0.5$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Joint Probability</StepTitle>

    The sample space is $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$. 
    $H_1 \cap H_2 = \{HH\} \implies P(H_1 \cap H_2) = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Test the Independence Condition</StepTitle>

    Check if $P(H_1 \cap H_2) = P(H_1)P(H_2)$:
    $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$. 
    Since $0.25 = 0.25$, the condition holds. The events are **independent**.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
