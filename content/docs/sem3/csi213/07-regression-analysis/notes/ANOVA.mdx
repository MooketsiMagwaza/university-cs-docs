## 7.2 ANOVA Identity, Correlation, and Determination

To evaluate how well our regression line fits the data, we decompose the total variation in the dependent variable into explained and unexplained components. This forms the basis of the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) framework.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 7.2.1: The ANOVA Identity"
  description="The total variation in $Y$ can be partitioned into variation explained by the regression model and unexplained residual variation."
>

$$
SST = SSR + SSE
$$

Where:
- **Total Sum of Squares (SST):** $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2$ (Total variation in observed data)
- **Regression Sum of Squares (SSR):** $\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2$ (Variation explained by the model)
- **Error Sum of Squares (SSE):** $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ (Unexplained residual variation)

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: Algebraic Derivation of the ANOVA Identity</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Decompose the Deviation from the Mean</StepTitle>

    Add and subtract the predicted value $\hat{y}_i$ inside the squared term:
    $y_i - \bar{y} = (y_i - \hat{y}_i) + (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})$
    Let $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ (the residual). Then:
    $y_i - \bar{y} = e_i + (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Square Both Sides and Sum</StepTitle>

    $\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = \sum [e_i + (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})]^2$
    Expanding the square:
    $SST = \sum e_i^2 + \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 + 2\sum e_i(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})$
    $SST = SSE + SSR + 2\sum e_i(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Prove the Cross-Product Term Vanishes</StepTitle>

    Recall that $\hat{y}_i - \bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_1(x_i - \bar{x})$. The cross-term becomes:
    $2\hat{\beta}_1 \sum e_i (x_i - \bar{x}) = 2\hat{\beta}_1 \left[ \sum e_i x_i - \bar{x} \sum e_i \right]$
    From the OLS normal equations, we know $\sum e_i = 0$ and $\sum e_i x_i = 0$ (residuals are orthogonal to the predictors and sum to zero).
    Therefore, the cross-product term equals $0$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Conclude the Identity</StepTitle>

    With the cross-term eliminated, we are left with:
    $SST = SSR + SSE$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 7.2.2: Pearson Correlation Coefficient"
  description="Measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables."
>

$$
r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
$$

Properties: $-1 \le r \le 1$. Sign matches $\hat{\beta}_1$. $|r|=1$ indicates perfect linear relationship.

</HighlightCard>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 7.2.3: Coefficient of Determination"
  description="Represents the proportion of the total variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variable(s)."
>

$$
R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}
$$

In **simple linear regression**, $R^2$ is exactly the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient:
$$
R^2 = r^2
$$

</HighlightCard>

<Card>

### Definition 7.2.1 (The Interpretation Trap)

Students frequently misinterpret $R^2$ and correlation on exams. Avoid these critical traps:

1. **High $R^2$ $\neq$ Good Model:** A high $R^2$ only indicates a strong linear fit. It does not prove the linear model is appropriate (e.g., the true relationship could be quadratic), nor does it validate regression assumptions (homoscedasticity, independence, normality of errors).
2. **Correlation $\neq$ Causation:** A high $|r|$ or high $R^2$ indicates association, not a causal mechanism. Confounding variables or reverse causality may exist.
3. **Sensitivity to Outliers:** Both $r$ and $R^2$ are highly sensitive to outliers and leverage points. A single influential observation can artificially inflate or deflate $R^2$.
4. **Adding Predictors Inflates $R^2$:** In multiple regression, adding any variable (even irrelevant ones) will never decrease $R^2$. Use **Adjusted $R^2$** for model comparison.

</Card>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Calculating and Interpreting $R^2$</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A regression model yields $SST = 1500$ and $SSE = 450$. Calculate $R^2$ and interpret its meaning.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate $R^2$</StepTitle>

    $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} = 1 - \frac{450}{1500} = 1 - 0.30 = 0.70$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Formal Interpretation</StepTitle>

    "70% of the variation in the dependent variable is explained by the linear relationship with the independent variable. 30% of the variation remains unexplained (residual)."
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Interpretation Trap Check</StepTitle>

    Before concluding the model is "good," we must verify linearity via residual plots, check for influential points, and ensure the assumptions of OLS are met. $R^2 = 0.70$ alone is insufficient for model validation.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
