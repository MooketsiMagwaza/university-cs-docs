## 4.2 Expectation and Variance

Once a random variable is defined, we need metrics to describe its central tendency and its spread. Expectation (mean) and variance are the two most fundamental descriptors of a probability distribution.

<Card>

### Definition 4.2.1 (Expectation / Mean)

The **Expected Value** (or Mean), denoted $E(X)$ or $\mu$, represents the long-run average value of the random variable.

- **For Discrete $X$:** 
  $$ E(X) = \sum_{x} x \cdot p(x) $$
- **For Continuous $X$:** 
  $$ E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x) \, dx $$

</Card>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 4.2.1: Linearity of Expectation"
  description="Expectation is a linear operator. This holds true regardless of whether the random variables are independent or dependent."
>

For any constants $a$ and $b$, and any random variable $X$:
$$
E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: Linearity of Expectation (Discrete Case)</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Apply the Definition of Expectation</StepTitle>

    Let $Y = aX + b$. By definition, $E[Y] = \sum_{x} (ax + b) \cdot p(x)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Distribute the Summation</StepTitle>

    $E[aX + b] = \sum_{x} (ax \cdot p(x) + b \cdot p(x))$
    $E[aX + b] = \sum_{x} ax \cdot p(x) + \sum_{x} b \cdot p(x)$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Factor out Constants</StepTitle>

    $E[aX + b] = a \sum_{x} x \cdot p(x) + b \sum_{x} p(x)$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Apply PMF Validity Conditions</StepTitle>

    We know $\sum_{x} x \cdot p(x) = E[X]$ and $\sum_{x} p(x) = 1$.
    Substituting these yields:
    $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b(1) = aE[X] + b$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

*(Note: The proof for the continuous case is identical in structure, replacing the summation $\sum$ with the integral $\int$ and $p(x)$ with $f(x)$).*

### Variance and Standard Deviation

While expectation measures the center of a distribution, variance measures its dispersion or "spread" around that center.

<Card>

### Definition 4.2.2 (Variance)

The **Variance** of a random variable $X$, denoted $V(X)$ or $\sigma^2$, is the expected value of the squared deviation from the mean $\mu = E[X]$.

$$
V(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2]
$$

The **Standard Deviation**, denoted $\sigma$, is simply the positive square root of the variance: $\sigma = \sqrt{V(X)}$.

</Card>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 4.2.2: Computational Formula for Variance"
  description="A mathematically equivalent but computationally much simpler formula for calculating variance."
>

$$
V(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: The Computational Formula for Variance</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Start with the Definition</StepTitle>

    $V(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2]$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Expand the Squared Term</StepTitle>

    $V(X) = E[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2]$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Linearity of Expectation</StepTitle>

    Expectation is a linear operator, so we can distribute it across the addition/subtraction, and treat $\mu$ and $\mu^2$ as constants:
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - E[2\mu X] + E[\mu^2]$
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - 2\mu E[X] + \mu^2$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Substitute $E[X] = \mu$</StepTitle>

    Since $\mu$ is defined as $E[X]$, we substitute it into the middle term:
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - 2\mu(\mu) + \mu^2$
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2$
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$
    
    Replacing $\mu$ with $E[X]$ gives the final computational formula:
    $V(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Calculating Expectation and Variance</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with PMF: $p(1) = 0.2$, $p(2) = 0.5$, $p(3) = 0.3$. Find $E[X]$ and $V(X)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate $E[X]$</StepTitle>

    $E[X] = \sum x \cdot p(x) = (1)(0.2) + (2)(0.5) + (3)(0.3)$
    $E[X] = 0.2 + 1.0 + 0.9 = 2.1$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate $E[X^2]$</StepTitle>

    $E[X^2] = \sum x^2 \cdot p(x) = (1^2)(0.2) + (2^2)(0.5) + (3^2)(0.3)$
    $E[X^2] = (1)(0.2) + (4)(0.5) + (9)(0.3)$
    $E[X^2] = 0.2 + 2.0 + 2.7 = 4.9$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Apply the Computational Variance Formula</StepTitle>

    $V(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$
    $V(X) = 4.9 - (2.1)^2 = 4.9 - 4.41 = 0.49$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
