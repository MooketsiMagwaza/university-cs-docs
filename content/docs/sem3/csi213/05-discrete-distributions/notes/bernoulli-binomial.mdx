## 5.1 Bernoulli and Binomial Distributions

The Bernoulli and Binomial distributions are the cornerstone models for processes involving repeated, independent trials with two possible outcomes (success or failure).

<Card>

### Definition 5.1.1 (Bernoulli Distribution)

A random variable $X$ follows a **Bernoulli Distribution** with parameter $p$ (where $0 \le p \le 1$), denoted $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, if it represents the outcome of a single trial:
$$
p(x) = 
\begin{cases} 
p & \text{if } x = 1 \text{ (Success)} \\
1-p & \text{if } x = 0 \text{ (Failure)}
\end{cases}
$$
This can be written compactly as $p(x) = p^x (1-p)^{1-x}$ for $x \in \{0, 1\}$.
Its moments are $E[X] = p$ and $V[X] = p(1-p)$.

</Card>

<Card>

### Definition 5.1.2 (Binomial Distribution)

A random variable $X$ follows a **Binomial Distribution** with parameters $n$ (number of trials) and $p$ (probability of success), denoted $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$, if it counts the total number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials.

**Probability Mass Function (PMF):**
$$
p(x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} \quad \text{for } x = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n
$$

</Card>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: Binomial PMF Sums to 1</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>State the Goal</StepTitle>

    We must prove that $\sum_{x=0}^n \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} = 1$ to satisfy the normalization condition of a valid PMF.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Invoke the Binomial Theorem</StepTitle>

    The Binomial Theorem states that for any real numbers $a$ and $b$, and integer $n \ge 0$:
    $(a + b)^n = \sum_{x=0}^n \binom{n}{x} a^x b^{n-x}$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Substitute Parameters</StepTitle>

    Let $a = p$ and $b = (1-p)$. Substituting these into the theorem yields:
    $\sum_{x=0}^n \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} = (p + (1-p))^n$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Simplify and Conclude</StepTitle>

    Since $p + (1-p) = 1$, the expression simplifies to $1^n = 1$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 5.1.1: Moments of the Binomial Distribution"
  description="The expectation and variance of a Binomial random variable, derived elegantly using the linearity of expectation."
>

For $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$:
$$
E[X] = np \quad \text{and} \quad V[X] = np(1-p)
$$

**Derivation Insight:** A Binomial random variable can be expressed as the sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli random variables: $X = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n$, where $X_i \sim \text{Bern}(p)$. 
By Linearity of Expectation: $E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n E[X_i] = \sum_{i=1}^n p = np$.
Because the trials are independent, variances add: $V[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n V[X_i] = \sum_{i=1}^n p(1-p) = np(1-p)$.

</HighlightCard>
