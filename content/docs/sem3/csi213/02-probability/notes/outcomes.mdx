## 2.3 Equally Likely Outcomes and Combinatorial Probability

When a random experiment has a finite sample space and no outcome is favored over another, we can determine probabilities purely through counting. This is known as classical or combinatorial probability.

<Card>

### Definition 2.3.1 (Equally Likely Outcomes)

A finite sample space $S$ consists of **Equally Likely Outcomes** if every individual sample point $\omega \in S$ has the exact same probability of occurring. 

If $|S| = N$ (the total number of outcomes), then the probability of any single outcome $\omega$ is:
$$
P(\{\omega\}) = \frac{1}{N}
$$

</Card>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.3.1: Classical Probability Formula"
  description="The fundamental formula for calculating the probability of an event in a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes."
>

$$
P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|S|} = \frac{n(A)}{N}
$$

Where:
- $n(A)$ is the number of favorable outcomes for event $A$.
- $N$ is the total number of possible outcomes in the sample space $S$.

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example 1: Basic Combinatorial Probability</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    Two fair 6-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum of the two dice is exactly 7?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Determine the Sample Space Size ($N$)</StepTitle>

    By the Product Rule, the total number of outcomes is $6 \times 6 = 36$. Thus, $N = 36$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Determine the Favorable Outcomes ($n(A)$)</StepTitle>

    Let $A$ be the event "sum is 7". The favorable outcomes are:
    $A = \{(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)\}$.
    Thus, $n(A) = 6$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Probability</StepTitle>

    $P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{N} = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example 2: Advanced Combinatorial Probability</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A 5-card hand is dealt from a standard, well-shuffled 52-card deck. What is the probability of being dealt a "Full House" (three cards of one rank, and two cards of another rank)?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Determine the Sample Space Size ($N$)</StepTitle>

    The total number of ways to choose 5 cards from 52, where order does not matter, is:
    $N = \binom{52}{5} = 2,598,960$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Determine Favorable Outcomes ($n(A)$) via Product Rule</StepTitle>

    To form a Full House, we must perform sequential choices:
    1. Choose the rank for the three-of-a-kind: $\binom{13}{1} = 13$ ways.
    2. Choose 3 suits out of 4 for that rank: $\binom{4}{3} = 4$ ways.
    3. Choose the rank for the pair from the remaining 12 ranks: $\binom{12}{1} = 12$ ways.
    4. Choose 2 suits out of 4 for that rank: $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ ways.
    
    Total favorable outcomes: $n(A) = 13 \times 4 \times 12 \times 6 = 3,744$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate Probability</StepTitle>

    $P(\text{Full House}) = \frac{3,744}{2,598,960} \approx 0.00144$.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example 3: Strategic Application of the Complement Rule</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>

    A committee of 4 people is randomly selected from a group of 10 people, which includes 6 men and 4 women. What is the probability that the committee includes **at least one** woman?
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Identify the Strategy</StepTitle>

    Calculating "at least one" directly requires summing the probabilities of 1, 2, 3, or 4 women. It is computationally simpler to use the Complement Rule (Theorem 2.2.3):
    $P(\text{at least one woman}) = 1 - P(\text{no women})$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Calculate the Complement Event</StepTitle>

    "No women" means all 4 members are selected from the 6 men.
    Total ways to choose 4 people: $N = \binom{10}{4} = 210$.
    Ways to choose 4 men: $n(\text{no women}) = \binom{6}{4} = 15$.
    $P(\text{no women}) = \frac{15}{210} = \frac{1}{14}$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Apply the Complement Rule</StepTitle>

    $P(\text{at least one woman}) = 1 - \frac{1}{14} = \frac{13}{14} \approx 0.9286$.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
