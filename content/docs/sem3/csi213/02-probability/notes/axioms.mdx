## 2.2 The Axioms of Probability and Fundamental Theorems

Modern probability is grounded in measure theory. In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov established that probability is a function that maps events (sets) to real numbers, provided that the function obeys three unbreakable rules. Every calculation in probability theory is a direct mathematical consequence of these axioms.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Axioms 2.2.1: The Kolmogorov Axioms"
  description="The foundational rules governing any valid probability measure $P$ on a sample space $S$."
>

**Axiom I (Non-negativity):** The probability of any event is a non-negative real number.
$$
P(A) \ge 0
$$

**Axiom II (Normalization):** The probability of the entire sample space is exactly 1.
$$
P(S) = 1
$$

**Axiom III (Countable Additivity):** If events $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive ($A \cap B = \emptyset$), the probability of their union is the sum of their individual probabilities.
$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)
$$
*(Note: In rigorous measure theory, this extends to a countably infinite sequence of pairwise disjoint events).*

</HighlightCard>

---

### Consequences of the Axioms

Because the three axioms act as the absolute source of truth, we must be able to use them to mathematically prove every other rule of probability.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.2.2: Probability of the Impossible Event"
  description="The probability of the empty set is strictly zero."
>

$$
P(\emptyset) = 0
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: The Impossible Event</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Define the Union</StepTitle>
    The sample space $S$ and the empty set $\emptyset$ are mutually exclusive by definition, and their union is the sample space itself: $S \cup \emptyset = S$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom III (Additivity)</StepTitle>
    Because they are disjoint, we apply the third axiom: $P(S \cup \emptyset) = P(S) + P(\emptyset)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom II and Conclude</StepTitle>
    Substitute $P(S \cup \emptyset)$ with $P(S)$. This yields: $P(S) = P(S) + P(\emptyset)$.
    Since Axiom II states $P(S) = 1$, we get: $1 = 1 + P(\emptyset) \implies P(\emptyset) = 0$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.2.3: The Complement Rule"
  description="The probability of an event's complement is 1 minus the probability of the event. This also proves that no probability can exceed 1."
>

$$
P(\overline{A}) = 1 - P(A) \quad \text{and consequently} \quad P(A) \le 1
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: The Complement Rule and Upper Bound</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Partition the Sample Space</StepTitle>
    An event $A$ and its complement $\overline{A}$ are mutually exclusive ($A \cap \overline{A} = \emptyset$) and together they completely cover the sample space: $S = A \cup \overline{A}$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axioms II and III</StepTitle>
    By Axiom II, $P(S) = 1$. By Axiom III, $P(A \cup \overline{A}) = P(A) + P(\overline{A})$.
    Equating them gives: $1 = P(A) + P(\overline{A})$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Isolate and Prove Bound</StepTitle>
    Rearranging yields the complement rule: $P(\overline{A}) = 1 - P(A)$.
    By Axiom I, $P(\overline{A}) \ge 0$. Substituting the complement formula gives $1 - P(A) \ge 0$, which rearranges to $P(A) \le 1$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.2.4: The Partitioning Rule"
  description="An event can be partitioned into the sum of its intersections with another event and its complement."
>

$$
P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap \overline{B})
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: The Partitioning Rule</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Deconstruct Set A</StepTitle>
    We can split event $A$ into two distinct, mutually exclusive parts: the part that overlaps with $B$, and the part that does not.
    $A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap \overline{B})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom III</StepTitle>
    Since $(A \cap B)$ and $(A \cap \overline{B})$ are disjoint, we apply Additivity:
    $P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap \overline{B})$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.2.5: Monotonicity"
  description="If the occurrence of event A mathematically guarantees the occurrence of event B, then A cannot be more probable than B."
>

$$
\text{If } A \subseteq B, \text{ then } P(A) \le P(B)
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: Monotonicity</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Deconstruct Set B</StepTitle>
    Since $A \subseteq B$, event $B$ consists of $A$ exactly, plus whatever part of $B$ is not in $A$.
    $B = A \cup (B \cap \overline{A})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom III</StepTitle>
    These two components are disjoint. By Additivity:
    $P(B) = P(A) + P(B \cap \overline{A})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom I</StepTitle>
    Axiom I dictates that $P(B \cap \overline{A}) \ge 0$. Since $P(B)$ is equal to $P(A)$ plus some non-negative value, $P(B)$ must be at least as large as $P(A)$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 2.2.6: The General Addition Rule"
  description="To find the probability of a union of overlapping events, we must correct for the intersection to avoid double-counting."
>

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
$$

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Proof: General Addition Rule</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Partition the Union</StepTitle>
    We can write the union of $A$ and $B$ as event $A$, plus the portion of $B$ that does *not* overlap with $A$.
    $A \cup B = A \cup (B \cap \overline{A})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Apply Axiom III</StepTitle>
    Because $A$ and $(B \cap \overline{A})$ are mutually exclusive:
    $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B \cap \overline{A})$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Substitute the Partitioning Rule</StepTitle>
    From Theorem 2.2.4 (applied to set B), we know that $P(B \cap \overline{A}) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
    Substituting this into the equation from Step 2 gives the final result:
    $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
