## 2.1 Sample Spaces and Events

Probability theory begins with the formalization of uncertainty. Before assigning numerical probabilities, we must define the universe of possible outcomes and the specific occurrences we wish to measure.

<Card>

### Definition 2.1.1 (Random Experiment)

A **Random Experiment** is a process or procedure that can be repeated under identical conditions, yields a well-defined set of possible outcomes, but for which the specific outcome of any single trial cannot be predicted with certainty.

</Card>

<Card>

### Definition 2.1.2 (Sample Space)

The **Sample Space**, denoted by $S$ (or $\Omega$), is the set of all possible distinct outcomes of a random experiment. Each individual outcome is called a sample point.

</Card>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Identifying Sample Spaces</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Experiment 1: Coin Toss</StepTitle>
    Tossing a single fair coin. The sample space is $S = \{H, T\}$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Experiment 2: Die Roll</StepTitle>
    Rolling a standard 6-sided die. The sample space is $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Experiment 3: Continuous Measurement</StepTitle>
    Measuring the time (in seconds) until a lightbulb fails. The sample space is $S = \{t \in \mathbb{R} \mid t \ge 0\}$.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

<Card>

### Definition 2.1.3 (Event)

An **Event**, typically denoted by uppercase letters ($A, B, C, \dots$), is any subset of the sample space $S$. An event is said to have occurred if the outcome of the experiment is an element of that subset.
- The **Certain Event** is the entire sample space $S$.
- The **Impossible Event** is the empty set $\emptyset$.

</Card>

<Card>

### Definition 2.1.4 (Mutually Exclusive Events)

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **Mutually Exclusive** (or disjoint) if they cannot occur simultaneously. Mathematically, this means their intersection is the empty set:
$$
A \cap B = \emptyset
$$
This concept extends to a collection of events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$, which are mutually exclusive if $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$.

</Card>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Example: Identifying Mutually Exclusive Events</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Problem Statement</StepTitle>
    A single card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. Let $A$ be the event "drawing a Heart" and $B$ be the event "drawing a King". Let $C$ be the event "drawing a Spade".
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Analyze Events A and B</StepTitle>
    The intersection $A \cap B$ is the event "drawing the King of Hearts". Since this is a valid outcome, $A \cap B \neq \emptyset$. Therefore, $A$ and $B$ are **not** mutually exclusive.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Analyze Events A and C</StepTitle>
    A single card cannot be both a Heart and a Spade simultaneously. Therefore, $A \cap C = \emptyset$. Events $A$ and $C$ **are** mutually exclusive.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
