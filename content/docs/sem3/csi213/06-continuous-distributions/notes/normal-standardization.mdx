## 6.3 The Normal Distribution and Standardization

The normal distribution models measurements produced by many small, roughly independent effects. It is useful for heights, measurement error, manufacturing dimensions and many aggregate quantities. It is symmetric and unbounded, so it is most appropriate when values cluster around a centre and extreme values become progressively less likely on both sides.

## 1. Definition and geometry

<Card>

### Definition 6.3.1 — Normal distribution

A random variable $X$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, written

$$X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),$$

if its PDF is

$$
f(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
e^{-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2},
\qquad -\infty<x<\infty,
$$

where $-\infty<\mu<\infty$ and $\sigma>0$.

</Card>

The curve has these defining features:

- it is symmetric about $x=\mu$;
- mean, median and mode all equal $\mu$;
- total area under the curve is $1$;
- the curve approaches, but never touches, the horizontal axis;
- $\mu$ controls location;
- $\sigma$ controls spread: a larger $\sigma$ creates a wider, lower curve.

<Callout type="warn" title="The second normal parameter is the variance">
In $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, the second parameter is variance. The standardisation formula divides by the standard deviation $\sigma$, not by the variance $\sigma^2$.
</Callout>

## 2. Is a normal model reasonable?

A normal approximation is plausible when the variable is continuous, roughly symmetric, unimodal, and not strongly restricted near its mean. It may be poor for strongly skewed waiting times, counts near zero, or lifetimes with a long right tail.

A quick plausibility check is to see whether $\mu-3\sigma$ and $\mu+3\sigma$ fit the physical context. For a variable that cannot be negative, a normal model with substantial area below zero is questionable.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.3.1 — Empirical rule"
  description="The same proportions lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean for every normal distribution."
>

$$P(\mu-\sigma<X<\mu+\sigma)\approx0.6827,$$

$$P(\mu-2\sigma<X<\mu+2\sigma)\approx0.9545,$$

$$P(\mu-3\sigma<X<\mu+3\sigma)\approx0.9973.$$

</HighlightCard>

These are useful for estimating and checking, but use the Z-table for a requested accurate probability.

## 3. Standard normal distribution

<Card>

### Definition 6.3.2 — Standard normal distribution

The standard normal random variable is

$$Z\sim N(0,1),$$

with PDF

$$\phi(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$$

and CDF

$$\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(t)\,dt.$$

</Card>

The integral defining $\Phi$ has no elementary antiderivative. Numerical values are therefore provided by a Z-table or calculator. The CSI213 table uses **left-tail cumulative areas**, so a table entry is $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.3.2 — Standardisation"
  description="Subtracting the mean measures displacement from the centre; dividing by the standard deviation expresses that displacement in standard-deviation units."
>

If $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then

$$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1).$$

Conversely,

$$X=\mu+Z\sigma.$$

</HighlightCard>

A Z-score of $1.5$ means the observation is $1.5$ standard deviations above the mean. A Z-score of $-2$ means it is $2$ standard deviations below the mean.

## 4. Reading a cumulative Z-table

For $z=1.23$:

1. find row $1.2$;
2. move to column $0.03$;
3. read the entry $0.8907$;
4. interpret it as $P(Z\le1.23)=0.8907$.

For a negative value such as $z=-1.23$, either use the negative rows of a full table or symmetry:

$$\Phi(-1.23)=1-\Phi(1.23)=1-0.8907=0.1093.$$

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.3.3 — Symmetry identities"
  description="Reflecting an interval across zero preserves its area."
>

$$\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z),$$

$$P(Z>z)=1-\Phi(z)=\Phi(-z),$$

$$P(-z<Z<z)=2\Phi(z)-1\quad(z>0).$$

</HighlightCard>

## 5. Forward problems: value to probability

The general procedure is:

1. sketch and shade the requested region;
2. standardise every raw boundary;
3. rewrite the event in terms of $Z$;
4. use the table's left-tail areas;
5. subtract or complement as required.

### Left tail

$$
P(X\le x)=P\left(Z\le\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
=\Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
$$

### Right tail

$$
P(X>x)=1-\Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
$$

### Between two values

$$
P(a<X<b)=
\Phi\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
-\Phi\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
$$

### Worked example 1 — probability between two lifetimes

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Bulb lifetimes are normal with mean 1200 hours and standard deviation 100 hours. Find the probability that a bulb lasts between 1100 and 1350 hours.</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>State the model and target</StepTitle>

    $X\sim N(1200,100^2)$ and the target is $P(1100<X<1350)$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Standardise both bounds</StepTitle>

    $$z_1=\frac{1100-1200}{100}=-1.00,$$

    $$z_2=\frac{1350-1200}{100}=1.50.$$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Convert to left-tail areas</StepTitle>

    $$P(-1<Z<1.5)=\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(-1).$$

    From the table, $\Phi(1.5)=0.9332$ and $\Phi(-1)=0.1587$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Subtract and interpret</StepTitle>

    $$P(1100<X<1350)=0.9332-0.1587=\boxed{0.7745}.$$

    About $77.45\%$ of bulbs lie in the stated lifetime interval.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

### Worked example 2 — a right tail

Suppose packet latency is $X\sim N(80,12^2)$ milliseconds. Then

$$
P(X>100)=P\left(Z>\frac{100-80}{12}\right)
=P(Z>1.67)
=1-\Phi(1.67).
$$

Using $\Phi(1.67)=0.9525$,

$$P(X>100)=\boxed{0.0475}.$$

The complement is essential because the table gives the area **left** of $1.67$, while the question asks for the area to the right.

## 6. Inverse problems: probability to value

Inverse questions give an area and ask for a raw cut-off. Use the process in the opposite direction:

1. convert the wording to a left-tail probability;
2. find the corresponding $z$ from the table;
3. un-standardise with $x=\mu+z\sigma$;
4. check whether the cut-off should lie above or below the mean.

### Worked example 3 — upper 5% cut-off

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>For $X\sim N(1200,100^2)$, find $x$ such that only 5% of bulbs last longer than $x$.</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Convert right-tail area to left-tail area</StepTitle>

    $$P(X>x)=0.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(X\le x)=0.95.$$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Find the standard-normal quantile</StepTitle>

    The table area closest to $0.9500$ gives $z\approx1.645$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Un-standardise</StepTitle>

    $$x=\mu+z\sigma=1200+(1.645)(100)=\boxed{1364.5\text{ hours}}.$$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Check direction</StepTitle>

    An upper-tail cut-off of only $5\%$ must lie above the mean. Since $1364.5>1200$, the direction is sensible.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

### Central percentages

To capture the central proportion $c$, split the excluded area equally between the two tails. For the central $90\%$,

$$\text{each tail}=\frac{1-0.90}{2}=0.05.$$

Thus the upper boundary uses left area $0.95$, so $z\approx1.645$, and the central interval is

$$\mu-1.645\sigma<X<\mu+1.645\sigma.$$

## 7. Linear transformations of normal variables

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.3.4 — Closure under linear transformations"
  description="A linear transformation of a normal variable is still normal. Transform the mean with aμ+b and the variance with a²σ²."
>

If $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $Y=aX+b$, then

$$Y\sim N(a\mu+b,a^2\sigma^2).$$

</HighlightCard>

For example, if Celsius temperature $C\sim N(20,3^2)$ and $F=\frac95C+32$, then

$$E[F]=\frac95(20)+32=68,$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(F)=\left(\frac95\right)^2(3^2)=29.16,$$

so

$$F\sim N(68,29.16),$$

with standard deviation $5.4$ degrees Fahrenheit.

## 8. Sums and averages of independent normal variables

If independent variables $X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)$, then

$$\sum_iX_i\sim N\left(\sum_i\mu_i,\sum_i\sigma_i^2\right).$$

For an independent sample $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ from $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$,

$$\bar X\sim N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right),$$

so the standard error of the mean is $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Do not divide variance by $\sqrt n$; it is the **standard deviation** that is divided by $\sqrt n$.

## 9. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Dividing the Z-score by $\sigma^2$ | Standardisation uses a standard deviation | Use $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ |
| Reading $\Phi(z)$ as a right-tail area | The course table is cumulative from the left | Right tail $=1-\Phi(z)$ |
| Subtracting interval areas in the wrong order | Probability becomes negative | Larger-bound CDF minus smaller-bound CDF |
| Looking up $0.05$ for the top $5\%$ | Top $5\%$ means left area $0.95$ | Complement before finding $z$ |
| Treating $P(X=x)$ as $f(x)$ | A continuous point has zero area | $P(X=x)=0$ |
| Rounding Z-scores too early | Table error can change the final answer | Keep extra digits until the table lookup or final step |
