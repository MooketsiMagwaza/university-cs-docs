## 6.1 Continuous Random Variables, PDFs and CDFs

A discrete random variable assigns probability to separate values. A continuous random variable can take every real value in an interval, so probability is assigned to **intervals**. The central idea is simple:

$$\boxed{\text{probability}=\text{area under a density curve}}$$

That change—from sums to integrals and from points to intervals—explains every formula in this note.

Examples of continuous measurements include lifetime, waiting time, mass, temperature and voltage. Even when an instrument rounds the reported measurement, the underlying model may still be continuous.

## 2. Probability density functions

<Card>

### Definition 6.1.1 — Probability density function

A function $f$ is a valid probability density function (PDF) of a continuous random variable $X$ if and only if:

$$f(x)\ge0\quad\text{for every }x,$$

and

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1.$$

For any $a<b$,

$$P(a<X<b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

</Card>

The value $f(x)$ is a **density**, not a probability. A density can be greater than $1$ as long as the total area is $1$. It also has units of “probability per unit of $x$”; integration multiplies density by width to produce a unitless probability.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.1.1 — A point has zero probability"
  description="An interval of zero width has zero area, even if the density at that point is positive."
>

$$P(X=c)=\int_c^c f(x)\,dx=0.$$

Therefore endpoint choices do not change continuous probabilities:

$$P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X<b)=P(a<X\le b)=P(a\le X\le b).$$

</HighlightCard>

<Callout type="warn" title="Do not substitute into a PDF to get a probability">
$f(2)$ is the curve's height at $2$; it is not $P(X=2)$. To find a probability, integrate over an interval or use the CDF.
</Callout>

### Small-interval approximation

For a very small positive width $\Delta x$, the curve is nearly flat, so

$$P(x<X<x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)\Delta x.$$

This explains the word *density*: probability is approximately density times width. It is an approximation, not the rule for an arbitrary large interval.

## 3. Integration toolkit

For $n\ne-1$,

$$\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C.$$

For a definite integral,

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a),$$

where $F'(x)=f(x)$. Useful exponential integrals are

$$\int e^{kx}\,dx=\frac1k e^{kx}+C\quad(k\ne0),$$

and

$$\int \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx=-e^{-\lambda x}+C.$$

When the PDF is piecewise, integrate only where it is non-zero and split an interval at every point where the formula changes.

## 4. Finding a normalising constant

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Suppose $f(x)=cx^2$ for $0\le x\le2$, and $f(x)=0$ otherwise. Find $c$ and then calculate $P(1<X<2)$.</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>Use total area 1</StepTitle>

    $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=\int_0^2cx^2\,dx=1.$$
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>Integrate and solve for c</StepTitle>

    $$c\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=c\frac83=1,\qquad c=\frac38.$$
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Integrate over the requested interval</StepTitle>

    $$
    P(1<X<2)=\int_1^2\frac38x^2\,dx
    =\frac38\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^2
    =\frac18(8-1)=\boxed{\frac78}.
    $$
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Check reasonableness</StepTitle>

    The density rises as $x^2$, so most area should lie near $2$. A probability of $0.875$ between $1$ and $2$ is therefore plausible.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

## 5. Cumulative distribution functions

<Card>

### Definition 6.1.2 — Cumulative distribution function

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of any random variable is

$$F(x)=P(X\le x).$$

For a continuous variable with PDF $f$,

$$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt.$$

The integration variable is written as $t$ so that $x$ can remain the upper limit.

</Card>

A CDF always satisfies:

- $0\le F(x)\le1$;
- $F$ never decreases;
- $F(x)\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$ and $F(x)\to1$ as $x\to\infty$;
- for a continuous distribution, $f(x)=F'(x)$ wherever the derivative exists.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.1.2 — Interval and tail probabilities from a CDF"
  description="The CDF is a running total of area to the left. Subtract two running totals to isolate an interval."
>

$$P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a),$$

$$P(X>b)=1-F(b).$$

</HighlightCard>

### Build the CDF for the normalised example

For $f(x)=\frac38x^2$ on $[0,2]$,

$$
F(x)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x<0,\\[4pt]
\displaystyle\int_0^x\frac38t^2\,dt=\frac{x^3}{8}, & 0\le x\le2,\\[8pt]
1, & x>2.
\end{cases}
$$

The three branches matter. A CDF is defined for every real number, not only on the support of the density.

## 6. Expectation and moments

<Card>

### Definition 6.1.3 — Expected value of a continuous random variable

Provided the integral exists,

$$E[X]=\mu=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f(x)\,dx.$$

More generally, for a function $g$,

$$E[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f(x)\,dx.$$

</Card>

The expectation is the density-weighted balance point. It need not be a value the variable is likely to equal; in fact, a continuous variable has probability zero of equalling any exact value.

Important special cases are the raw moments

$$E[X^k]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^k f(x)\,dx.$$

The second raw moment $E[X^2]$ is used to compute variance efficiently.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.1.3 — Variance shortcut"
  description="Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean, and expands into a form involving the first two raw moments."
>

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]
=E[X^2]-\mu^2.
$$

$$\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}.$$

</HighlightCard>

### Worked example — mean and variance from a PDF

Continue with $f(x)=\frac38x^2$ on $0\le x\le2$.

$$
E[X]=\int_0^2x\left(\frac38x^2\right)dx
=\frac38\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2
=\frac32.
$$

$$
E[X^2]=\int_0^2x^2\left(\frac38x^2\right)dx
=\frac38\left[\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^2
=\frac{12}{5}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{12}{5}-\left(\frac32\right)^2
=\frac{48}{20}-\frac{45}{20}=\boxed{\frac3{20}},
$$

and

$$\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\frac3{20}}\approx0.3873.$$

The mean $1.5$ lies inside the support $[0,2]$, and the standard deviation is smaller than the width $2$, so the result passes basic reasonableness checks.

## 7. Linear transformations

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Theorem 6.1.4 — Effect of a linear transformation"
  description="Adding a constant moves the centre but does not change spread; multiplying by a constant scales standard deviation by its absolute value and variance by its square."
>

If $Y=aX+b$, then

$$E[Y]=aE[X]+b,$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X),$$

$$\operatorname{SD}(Y)=|a|\operatorname{SD}(X).$$

</HighlightCard>

For example, if temperature $C$ in degrees Celsius has mean $20$ and variance $9$, then $F=\frac95C+32$ has

$$E[F]=\frac95(20)+32=68,$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(F)=\left(\frac95\right)^2(9)=29.16.$$

The added $32$ does not appear in the variance because shifting every observation equally does not change their distances from one another.
