## Logic and Inference

Logic gives us a precise language for conditions. A **proposition** is a declarative statement that is either true or false, but not both. We use letters such as $p$, $q$, and $r$ to represent propositions.

## Connectives

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\neg p$ | negation | not $p$ |
| $p \land q$ | conjunction | $p$ and $q$ |
| $p \lor q$ | disjunction | $p$ or $q$, possibly both |
| $p \oplus q$ | exclusive or | exactly one of $p$ and $q$ |
| $p \to q$ | implication | if $p$, then $q$ |
| $p \leftrightarrow q$ | biconditional | $p$ if and only if $q$ |

An implication is false only when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is false. This is why “if $p$, then $q$” does not say that $p$ causes $q$; it states a condition that must be preserved.

## Truth tables and equivalence

With $n$ variables there are $2^n$ rows. Build a compound table from the inside out, adding one column for each intermediate expression.

Two propositions are **logically equivalent**, written $p \equiv q$, when their truth values agree on every row. Important laws include:

$$
\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q, \qquad
\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q
$$

These are De Morgan's laws. A proposition true on every row is a **tautology**; one false on every row is a **contradiction**.

## Predicates and quantifiers

A **predicate** $P(x)$ becomes a proposition when a value is supplied for $x$. The universal statement $\forall x\,P(x)$ says every value in the domain satisfies $P$; the existential statement $\exists x\,P(x)$ says at least one does.

Negating quantifiers changes both the quantifier and the predicate:

$$
\neg(\forall x\,P(x)) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x), \qquad
\neg(\exists x\,P(x)) \equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x).
$$

Always state the domain. “Every student submitted” and “every integer submitted” are different mathematical claims.

## Rules of inference

An argument is valid when the conclusion follows from its premises. Common valid forms are:

- **Modus ponens:** $p \to q$ and $p$, therefore $q$.
- **Modus tollens:** $p \to q$ and $\neg q$, therefore $\neg p$.
- **Hypothetical syllogism:** $p \to q$ and $q \to r$, therefore $p \to r$.
- **Disjunctive syllogism:** $p \lor q$ and $\neg p$, therefore $q$.

Do not confuse a valid form with the converse. From $p \to q$ and $q$ you cannot conclude $p$; this is the fallacy of affirming the consequent.

## Common mistakes

- Treating “or” as exclusive when the problem allows both outcomes.
- Negating $p \to q$ as $\neg p \to \neg q$ instead of $p \land \neg q$.
- Omitting the domain when translating a quantified sentence.
- Assuming a statement is equivalent because it “sounds similar” without testing all rows or applying a law.
