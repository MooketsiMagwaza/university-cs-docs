## Writing and Referencing

Academic writing makes a claim, supports it with evidence, explains the connection, and acknowledges sources. It is a chain of reasoning rather than a collection of quotations.

## From question to argument

Unpack the assignment verb. **Describe** asks what or how; **compare** asks for similarities and differences; **analyse** asks how parts relate; **evaluate** asks for a supported judgement. Convert the prompt into a working question and answer it in one provisional sentence. That sentence becomes the argument to test during research.

## Paragraph structure

A reliable paragraph has a topic sentence, evidence, explanation, and link to the thesis or next idea. Evidence without explanation leaves the reader to do the reasoning; explanation without evidence becomes an unsupported opinion.

## Paraphrase and summary

To paraphrase, read for meaning, put the source aside, write the idea in a new structure and voice, then compare for accuracy. A summary reduces a larger passage to its essential claim and scope. Both require a citation because the underlying idea is borrowed. Direct quotations require quotation marks and an exact page or location when available.

## Revision and integrity

Revise in passes: argument and structure first, paragraph reasoning second, sentence clarity third, and proofreading last. Check that every in-text citation has a reference entry and every reference is used in the text.

Plagiarism includes presenting another person’s wording, ideas, structure, data, or creative work as your own. Common causes are copying notes too closely, missing quotation marks, and leaving citations until the end. Keep source material visually separate from your own commentary while researching.

Use the referencing style required by the course consistently. A reference should let a reader locate the source; include author or organisation, date, title, publication details, and a stable identifier or URL as the style requires.

<Callout type="warning" title="Final integrity check">
For each claim, ask whether you knew it before researching, derived it yourself, or received it from a source. If a source contributed it, cite it even when the sentence is entirely in your own words.
</Callout>
