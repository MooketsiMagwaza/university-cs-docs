## University Learning and Study Practice

University study is more self-directed than school study. Lectures introduce concepts, but learning is completed through preparation, practice, feedback, and reflection.

## Learning as a cycle

| Stage | Useful question | Evidence of progress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepare | What do I already know? | A short preview or question list |
| Engage | What is the central idea? | Annotated notes and examples |
| Practise | Can I use it without looking? | Solved problems or a summary from memory |
| Check | Where is my reasoning weak? | Marked work and error log |
| Reflect | What will I change next time? | A specific action for the next session |

Reading notes again feels familiar, but familiarity is not recall. Close the notes and reproduce a definition, diagram, proof outline, or program trace. This is **active recall**.

## Goals and time management

A useful goal is specific, observable, and time-bounded: “By Friday, I will solve ten induction exercises and correct every error” is stronger than “study discrete mathematics.” Break a course goal into weekly outcomes, then schedule sessions before the week becomes crowded.

For a study block, state the outcome, work for a focused interval with notifications removed, attempt questions before consulting a solution, record mistakes, and end by choosing the next starting point. Keep a small buffer for travel, group work, illness, and assignments. A timetable that uses every available minute is not robust.

## Working with lecturers and tutors

Ask questions that include your attempt: “I applied the product rule here, but my sign differs from the answer. Which step should I inspect?” This gives the tutor enough context to diagnose the misconception. Use office hours, consultation sessions, the library, writing support, and peer study groups early.

## Academic integrity

Your notes may combine ideas from lectures, books, websites, and classmates. Mark the source while taking notes so copied wording cannot later be mistaken for your own. Collaboration can help you understand a problem, but submitted work must represent your own reasoning and follow course rules.

## Common mistakes

- Scheduling time without defining what will be produced.
- Copying worked answers without attempting the question first.
- Waiting until an assessment to discover that a prerequisite is missing.
- Treating attendance as the same thing as learning.
- Keeping errors private instead of maintaining an error log.
