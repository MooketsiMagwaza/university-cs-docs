## Academic Reading

Academic reading is purposeful. The way you read a textbook definition, a research article, and an assignment brief should differ because each requires different evidence.

## Preview before reading closely

Start with the title, abstract or introduction, headings, figures, conclusion, and reference list. Predict the text’s argument and identify unfamiliar terms. This creates questions that guide close reading.

| Strategy | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Skimming | Find the overall structure and main claim |
| Scanning | Locate a particular name, term, number, or section |
| Close reading | Analyse reasoning, evidence, definitions, and assumptions |
| Critical reading | Evaluate reliability, limitations, and implications |

The SQ3R pattern—Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review—turns a long chapter into manageable passes. After each section, explain the idea without looking and write the evidence that supports it.

## Finding the main idea

A paragraph usually has a controlling idea supported by reasons, examples, data, or explanation. Ask: “What is this paragraph doing?” It may define a term, contrast two approaches, provide evidence, or draw a conclusion. Headings and signposting words reveal the author’s structure.

Read a figure’s caption, axes, units, legend, and source before interpreting it. For an equation, identify each variable, its domain, and the operation being described. Do not treat a visual as self-explanatory; state precisely what it shows and what it does not show.

## Critical questions

- What question is the author answering?
- What assumptions are made?
- Is the evidence relevant and sufficient?
- Are key terms defined consistently?
- What alternative explanation or limitation exists?
- Which claims can I use, and how will I cite them?

Annotate selectively with labels such as `claim`, `evidence`, `question`, and `connection`. A short synthesis in your own words is more useful than highlighting every sentence.
